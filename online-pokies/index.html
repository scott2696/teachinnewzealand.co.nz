Pokie types, and which suits how you play

Classic three-reel pokies

Three reels, one to five paylines, fruit and bar symbols. No bonus rounds, no free spins, nothing to learn. RTP is often high because there is nothing complex to fund. If you want the rhythm of a pub machine without the $2.50 cap, this is it. Fire Joker (Play'n GO) is the modern reference point.

Video pokies

Five or six reels, 10 to 50 paylines, wilds, scatters and a free-spins round. This is the default category and where most lobby depth sits. Good middle ground: enough happening to hold attention, variance mild enough that NZ$20 lasts an evening at 20-cent spins.

Megaways

Big Time Gaming's licensed mechanic. Each reel loads a variable number of symbols every spin, producing up to 117,649 ways to win. In practice it means high volatility and long dry runs punctuated by very large hits, almost always with a cascade mechanic in the free-spins round. Not a bankroll-friendly format — more on Megaways pokies.

Cluster pays and cascading reels

No paylines. Symbols pay when enough of them touch in a group, winners are removed, and new symbols drop in for a chance at a chain. Sweet Bonanza and Candy Jar Clusters are the well-known examples. Single spins can run for twenty seconds, which makes them feel generous even when they are not.

Bonus buy

Pay a fixed multiple of your stake — commonly 75× to 100× — to jump straight into the free-spins round. Bought rounds usually carry a slightly higher RTP than the base game, so the maths is not a rip-off. The behavioural risk is severe: a NZ$1 spin becomes a NZ$100 decision, and bankrolls disappear in minutes. Several jurisdictions have banned the feature outright. Treat it with real caution.

Jackpot pokies

Fixed jackpots pay a set amount. Progressives pool a slice of every stake across a network until someone wins. Progressive base-game RTP is always depressed to fund the pool, which is the trade you are making. See jackpot pokies.

Can you actually beat online pokies?

Nikau Te Aho · Lead Reviewer

I get asked this more than anything else, so I will be blunt. In three years of studio QA and two of building RTP databases I have never seen anything that beats a pokie, and I have looked properly. What follows is the small amount that genuinely is under your control, and the much longer list of things that are not.

No, and any page telling you otherwise is selling something. A pokie has a fixed negative expectation built into its maths. There is no bet pattern, time of day, stake progression or "loose machine" that changes it, because every spin is independent of every other spin.

What you can control is narrow but real:

  • Choose the return. Playing 96.8% instead of 94.2% is a 40% reduction in your expected loss rate. This is the single largest lever available to you and it costs nothing.
  • Match volatility to bankroll. High-volatility titles need roughly 200 spins to give the bonus round a fair chance to trigger. On NZ$20 that means 10-cent spins, not NZ$1 spins.
  • Refuse bad bonus terms. A bonus you cannot clear converts a withdrawable balance into a locked one. Declining is often the correct play.
  • Set the limit before you start. Deposit and loss limits in account settings are the only mechanism that reliably survives a bad run, because they do not depend on your judgement at the moment it is weakest.

Things that do not work, stated plainly: the Martingale and every other stake-progression system; "due" machines; switching titles after a loss; playing at quiet hours; autoplay patterns; and any product sold as a pokies system. Each is a restatement of the gambler's fallacy.

Pokies tournaments

Nikau Te Aho · Lead Reviewer

Almost uncontested ground in New Zealand — barely any competing guide covers tournaments, and they are one of the few ways to add value to spins you were making anyway.

The format: over a set window, players compete on a leaderboard measured by biggest single win multiplier, or by total wins, across a specified set of titles. Prizes split across the top finishers. Three things decide whether entering is worth anything:

  • Cash or bonus credit? A NZ$500 prize paid as bonus credit at 40× wagering is worth a fraction of NZ$500 cash. Spino pays most leaderboard prizes in cash, which is why it takes our tournament pick.
  • Multiplier-based or turnover-based? Multiplier leaderboards — biggest win relative to stake — are winnable on small stakes and are the only format worth a casual player's time. Turnover leaderboards simply reward whoever gambles most, and you will not out-stake a high roller.
  • What is the minimum qualifying spin? If it is NZ$1 and you normally play at NZ$0.20, entering means quintupling your stake. That cost is rarely worth the prize.

  4. Decide about the bonus with the arithmetic, not the banner

    Multiply the wagering by its base, then by 4% for a standard pokie. A NZ$100 bonus at 40× on deposit plus bonus costs about NZ$320 of expected loss to capture NZ$100. At small deposit sizes, declining is usually correct.

  6. Set your stake so the bankroll lasts 200+ spins

    NZ$20 at NZ$0.10 is 200 spins and an evening. NZ$20 at NZ$1 is twenty spins and four minutes. Stake size, not deposit size, decides how long you play.

    Cash out NZ$20–30 before you have a real balance riding on it. You learn how that cashier behaves at a moment when nothing depends on it.

My top tips for real-money pokies

Nikau Te Aho · Lead Reviewer

None of these will make you a winner — a pokie has a fixed negative expectation and nothing changes that. What they do is stop you losing more than you needed to, which is the only edge genuinely available.

1. Choose the return before you choose the theme

Playing a 96.8% title instead of a 94.2% one cuts your expected loss rate by roughly 40%. It is the single largest lever you control and it costs nothing. Open the information panel, read the RTP, and if it is materially below the studio's published figure, play the same game somewhere else.

2. Match volatility to bankroll, not to ambition

A high-volatility title needs roughly 200–300 spins to give its bonus round a fair chance to trigger. If your bankroll cannot sustain that at your chosen stake, you are paying for a feature you will not see. On NZ$50 that means NZ$0.20 a spin, not NZ$1.

3. Treat the max-bet rule as a hard boundary

If a bonus is active, exceeding the stake cap — usually NZ$5–8 — can void the bonus and everything won from it. It is a stated numeric rule and it is enforceable. Set the stake once and do not touch autoplay settings mid-session.

4. Withdraw winnings instead of leaving a float

A balance left on a site gets played back. That is the entire commercial logic of the 12–48 hour reversal window, and it works on almost everyone. Request the cashout and close the tab.

5. Ignore anything sold as a system

The random number generator cycles continuously and takes its value the instant you press spin. There is no memory between spins. Stake progressions, "due" machines, switching titles after a loss, playing at quiet hours — every one of these is a restatement of the gambler's fallacy, and the people selling them are the only ones profiting.

Are online pokies fair? How the maths is certified

Nikau Te Aho · Lead Reviewer

This is the question I get asked most and the one with the least satisfying honest answer. The games are fair. The thing that is not disclosed to you is which version of a fair game you are playing.

What the random number generator actually does

An RNG cycles continuously whether or not anyone is playing, generating values thousands of times a second. When you press spin it takes the value at that instant and maps it to a reel outcome. There is no memory between spins, no accumulator, and no awareness of your balance or session length. A machine is never "due", a title that has just paid is not "cold", and the time of day is irrelevant.

Certification sits with the studio, not the casino. Pragmatic Play certifies Gates of Olympus; the casino licenses a certified build and deploys it. That is why an operator cannot make an individual game pay less to you personally — it does not hold the code.

What is not disclosed, and this is the real issue

Studios certify multiple configurations of the same title — commonly 96.5%, 94.5% and sometimes 92%. All are fair. All are certified. The operator chooses which build to deploy, and you are told nowhere except the game's own information panel.

Across 440 checks at eleven casinos our analyst found 26 titles running below the studio standard. The best site ran none of forty; the worst ran six. Nothing on either casino's page distinguishes them. That is not rigging — it is legal, disclosed in the only place almost nobody looks, and it is the single strongest argument for checking the RTP before you spin. Full data on highest RTP pokies.

Provably fair, and what it does not cover

Some crypto-facing sites offer provably fair in-house games — dice, crash, plinko — where you can cryptographically verify the outcome was not altered after your bet. Genuinely useful, and it does not extend to third-party pokies, which run on the studio's certified RNG instead. Neither model is better; they are different assurance mechanisms. See crypto casinos.

New pokies releases and whether to play them

Studios ship several titles a month and lobbies compete on how fast they carry them. SlotsGem adds new releases faster than any site we track; the majors typically lag by two to six weeks on smaller studios.

Two practical notes on new titles. RTP tends to be at the studio standard on release and reduced builds appear later, so a genuinely new game is often the full-return version. And volatility has trended sharply upward over the last few years — max wins of 10,000× and above are now routine, which necessarily means longer dry runs to fund them. A 2018 release and a 2026 release at the same 96.4% RTP will feel nothing alike.

If you like the mechanics of a new title, demo it for 200 spins before committing. That is long enough to feel the base-game rhythm and to see whether the bonus round triggers at a rate your bankroll can survive.

The use nobody makes of it

How differently you behave with money at stake. In demo a losing run is a spectator experience; with NZ$50 of your own down, the same run produces an urge to raise stakes and keep going. Pub pokies and online pokies, compared

The caps your local machine is bound by

Under the Gambling (Harm Prevention and Minimisation) Regulations 2004, a Class 4 machine may not take more than NZ$2.50 per play or pay more than NZ$500 on a single play, with linked jackpots capped at NZ$1,000. Typical return is around 90%. None of those limits applies to an offshore online pokie.

Where online genuinely wins

Six percentage points of return, no stake or prize ceiling, free demo play, and four to seventeen thousand titles against whatever a venue has installed. On NZ$10,000 of turnover the RTP difference alone is around NZ$600.

Where the venue wins, and it matters

The Department of Internal Affairs regulates Class 4 and will take your complaint. Multi-Venue Exclusion covers every participating venue in a region with one request, where online self-exclusion stops at one operator's door. Venues close, staff are trained to intervene, and at least 40% of profit is legally required to return to community grants. Online has none of that.

The honest conclusion

Online is the better product and the riskier one, and both are true for the same reason: the caps are gone. A NZ$2.50 limit is a harm-minimisation device, and removing it removes a brake. How pokies changed between 2018 and 2026

Volatility has risen sharply

Max wins of 10,000× and above are now routine where 2,000× was once a big ceiling. That extra top end has to be funded from somewhere, and it is funded by longer dry runs in the base game. A 2018 release and a 2026 release at the same 96.4% RTP feel nothing alike, and the newer one needs considerably more bankroll to play properly.

Bonus buys arrived and became contentious

Paying 75–100× stake to skip straight to the feature was rare before 2019 and is now on a large share of new releases. The maths is defensible — bought rounds usually carry slightly higher RTP — and the behavioural risk is real enough that several jurisdictions have banned the mechanic outright.

Mechanics converged

Tumbling reels, pay-anywhere clusters and rising multipliers in free spins now appear across almost every studio. The variety in a 17,000-title lobby is narrower than the number implies; you are largely choosing between themes wrapped around four or five underlying maths models.

RTP transparency got worse, not better

Multiple certified configurations of the same title were uncommon a decade ago and are now standard practice. That is the single biggest change affecting what a New Zealand player actually receives, and it is disclosed only in the game information panel. Our sampling method exists because of it.

What is the difference between online pokies and pub pokies?

How do paylines, wilds and scatters work?

A payline is a defined path across the reels; matching symbols along it pay. A wild substitutes for most other symbols to complete a combination, and may expand, stack or carry a multiplier. A scatter pays regardless of position and usually triggers the free-spins round when three or more land. Cluster-pays titles have no paylines at all, paying instead when enough matching symbols touch in a group.

Can I improve my chances at pokies?

Only in two ways, both of which happen before you spin. Choose a higher RTP title, since 96.8% instead of 94.2% cuts your expected loss rate by about 40%. And match volatility to your bankroll, because a high-volatility title needs roughly 200 spins to give its bonus round a fair chance. No stake progression, timing pattern or system affects the outcome of a spin, since every spin is independent.

What does volatility mean on a pokie?

It describes how a title's return is distributed. Low volatility pays small amounts often, so your balance falls slowly and a NZ$20 deposit lasts. High volatility pays rarely but large, so most sessions end at zero and a few end at hundreds of times stake. Two pokies can both return 96% and feel completely different. Choose it to match your bankroll rather than treating it as a quality rating.

Can I play online pokies for free?

Yes. Nearly every title offers a demo mode with play-money credits and the identical maths and RTP as the real-money version. Many sites let you demo without registering at all. What is a bonus buy and should I use one?

A bonus buy lets you pay a fixed multiple of your stake, commonly 75 to 100 times, to enter the free-spins round immediately. The maths is generally fair, since bought rounds usually carry a slightly higher RTP than the base game. The behavioural risk is not: it converts a NZ$1 spin into a NZ$100 decision and can empty a bankroll in minutes. Several jurisdictions have banned the feature. Approach with real caution.

Do online pokies pay better at certain times?

No. The random number generator cycles continuously whether or not anyone is playing, and takes its value at the instant you press spin. There is no memory between spins, no busy-period adjustment and no time of day that changes the maths. Any advice built on timing is built on the gambler's fallacy.

What is the difference between pokies and slots?

Nothing but the word. Pokies is the New Zealand and Australian term for what the rest of the world calls slot machines, short for poker machines. The games, studios and mechanics are identical, and you will see both terms used in the same lobby because the interfaces are built for a global audience.

How many spins do I need for a Megaways bonus round?

Roughly 250 on average, though variance is enormous. That is why bankroll matters more than most players realise: on NZ$50 it means spinning at NZ$0.20, not NZ$1. Playing a Megaways title at a stake your bankroll cannot sustain for 250 spins is why people conclude they are tight when they are simply being played wrong.

What is an ante bet and is it worth using?

An optional side bet, usually 25% extra per spin, that doubles or improves your chance of triggering the bonus round. RTP typically rises slightly when it is active, so mathematically it is often marginally better than the base game — but it increases your cost per spin by a quarter, which shortens a fixed bankroll accordingly.

Are pokies tournaments worth entering?

Only if the prize is cash rather than bonus credit, the leaderboard is multiplier-based rather than turnover-based, and the minimum qualifying spin is at or below your normal stake. Turnover leaderboards simply reward whoever gambles most. Multiplier leaderboards are winnable on small stakes and are the only format worth a casual player's time.
