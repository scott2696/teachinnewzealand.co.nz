How progressive jackpots work

A slice of every stake — typically 1–3% — is diverted into a shared pool rather than paid back through the paytable. The pool grows across every player on the network until someone triggers it, then resets to a seed value. Networked progressives like Mega Moolah pool across many casinos simultaneously, which is why they reach the numbers they do.

The consequence follows directly: the base game must return less, because part of what would have been returned is going into the pool instead. Mega Moolah's base RTP sits around 88.1%. A standard pokie returns 96%. That eight-point gap is the jackpot's price, paid by every player who does not win it, which is essentially all of them.

Does a jackpot win bypass the withdrawal ceiling?

This is the question that actually matters and almost nobody answers it. Most operators exempt progressive jackpot wins from the standard daily and monthly ceilings, paying them as a lump sum or on a schedule set by the game's network operator rather than the casino.

But it is a written term, not a courtesy, and it varies. Find the clause before you play progressives. If you cannot find it, assume the ordinary ceiling applies — and at a site capped at NZ$15,000 a month, a NZ$500,000 jackpot would take nearly three years.

Ceilings, if the jackpot exemption does not apply

Tax on a jackpot win in New Zealand

None. Inland Revenue treats gambling winnings as a windfall, and a NZ$1 million jackpot is treated exactly as a NZ$10 win: no income tax, no GST, no threshold, no form. This is genuinely more generous than most countries — a US player would face federal withholding on the same win.

Two practical points. Interest the money earns once banked is taxable income like any other interest. And a very large deposit will trigger anti-money-laundering questions from your bank under the AML/CFT Act — keep the casino's withdrawal confirmation and transaction history so you can evidence the source immediately. Seeds, resets and why the size matters

A progressive has a seed value — the amount it resets to after a win — and grows from there at a fixed contribution rate. Mega Moolah seeds at around NZ$1m and climbs roughly NZ$1m a month depending on network volume.

This produces a genuine, if narrow, strategic point. The jackpot's expected value rises as the pool grows while your cost per spin stays flat. A pool well above its historical average drop point is, in pure expectation, a better bet than the same game at seed — because the prize is bigger and the odds are unchanged.

Two things stop that being useful advice. The base game is still returning 88%, so you are paying eight points above a normal pokie for the privilege. And the odds of any individual player hitting it remain on the order of tens of millions to one. Playing a large pool rather than a fresh one is marginally less bad, not good.

Fixed, must-drop and local jackpots

Fixed jackpots

A set top prize with no pool to fund, so base RTP stays near normal at 95–96%. You give up the life-changing headline in exchange for roughly eight percentage points of better return on every spin. For most players this is the better trade and it is heavily under-marketed because the numbers do not make good banners.

Must-drop jackpots

Guaranteed to pay before reaching a set value or by a set time — hourly or daily. The guarantee is real, but it does not improve your individual odds; it narrows the window in which someone wins. Base RTP is usually better than a networked progressive because the pool is smaller and funded less aggressively.

Local jackpots

Pooled within one operator rather than across a network. Smaller prizes, meaningfully better odds, and they drop far more often. If the appeal of a jackpot is the realistic possibility of winning one rather than the headline number, a local pool is where to look.

What happens after a large win

  1. The win is verified before it is paid

    Network progressives are validated by the game supplier, not the casino, which is why they can take days rather than minutes. This is normal and it is also why they are usually exempt from the casino's own withdrawal ceiling.

  2. Full identity and source-of-funds verification

    Expect photo ID, proof of address and, on a large win, evidence of where your deposit money came from. Intrusive, legally required, and unavoidable.

  3. Payment schedule is set by the network, not the site

    Some pay a lump sum; some pay in instalments over a defined period. This is a written term and it varies by network. Find it before you chase a jackpot, not after.

  4. No New Zealand tax is due on the win

    Inland Revenue treats gambling winnings as a windfall regardless of amount. Interest earned once banked is taxable, and your bank will ask about source of funds under AML rules — keep the withdrawal confirmation.

  5. Get it out of the casino

    An online casino balance is not protected savings and no offshore licensor covering NZ-facing sites runs a compensation scheme. Withdraw promptly.

The honest maths on chasing a progressive

A NZ$1 spin on Mega Moolah returns about NZ$0.88 in expectation against NZ$0.965 on a standard 96.5% pokie. Over 1,000 spins that is roughly NZ$85 of additional expected loss — the price of the ticket.

Whether that is worth paying is a personal question and not one arithmetic settles. What arithmetic does settle is that a progressive is a lottery attached to a below-average pokie, and that treating it as anything else — a strategy, a bankroll plan, a thing to grind — is a mistake. If you want the shot, buy it deliberately with a fixed amount you have decided to spend, then go back to a 96.5% title.

Jackpot pokies and bonus terms

Usually excluded from wagering

Most operators bar progressive jackpot titles from bonus play entirely, or weight them at zero. The logic is straightforward: the casino contributes to a pool it does not control, so letting bonus funds feed that pool costs it twice. Check the excluded games list before you start — playing an excluded title with bonus funds contributes nothing and at some operators voids the bonus.

The jackpot exemption on withdrawals

Most sites exempt progressive wins from the daily and monthly ceiling, paying instead on the network's schedule. It is a written term, it varies, and two of the sixteen sites could not point me to the clause when I asked in live chat. Get it in writing before you chase a pool, because at a NZ$15,000 monthly ceiling a NZ$500,000 win without an exemption takes nearly three years.

Minimum qualifying stake

Several networked progressives only pay the top tier at or above a minimum bet — commonly NZ$0.25 or NZ$0.50 a spin. Below it you are contributing to the pool without being eligible for the headline prize, which is the worst possible position. Check the qualifying stake in the paytable before your first spin.

Choosing between jackpot types

If you want the best odds of winning something

A local or daily-drop jackpot. Smaller pools, far better odds, and they pay out frequently enough that winning one is a realistic outcome rather than a fantasy. Base RTP is usually 94–96%, so you give up much less than a networked progressive costs you.

If you want a normal pokie with a big top end

A fixed jackpot. No pool to fund means base RTP stays near 95–96%, and the top prize is still substantial. This is the option most players should take and the one that gets marketed least, because the numbers do not make good banners.

If you specifically want the life-changing number

A networked progressive, bought deliberately with an amount you have decided to spend. Accept that you are paying roughly eight percentage points of return for a lottery ticket, set a limit, and go back to a 96.5% title afterwards.

Usually, but check first. Most operators exempt progressive jackpot wins from the standard daily and monthly withdrawal ceilings and pay them as a lump sum or on the game network's schedule.
